COMPREHENSIVE PLANNING COMMITTEE  
INFORMATION ITEM: Communities Regulating Data Centers 
Ms. Seitz provided a quick update on data center ordinances and the 24 communities that 
currently have adopted zoning regulations related to data centers or that are working on zoning 
regulations related to data centers, and the 25 municipalities that are covered because they are 
part of multi-municipal comprehensive plans with communities that are adopting regulations. So 
altogether, 49 of the 62 municipalities in the region are directing where data centers could 
possibly go and what standards they must follow. Ms. Seitz also briefed the Commission that 
the LVPC is not only evaluating whether municipalities have regulations but also how strong, 
clear and practical those regulations are. Ms. Seitz noted that several communities had adopted 
data center regulations and have immediately turned around and strengthened those 
ordinances to include provisions for heat impacts, decommissioning and setbacks, to name a 
few. Ms. Seitz also noted the LVPC has been providing extensive municipal and community 
support. The LVPC has heard from communities that the Lehigh County Industrial Land Use 
Guide, which followed the Northampton County Freight-Based Land Use Guide, continues to 
serve as a guiding document for this work. Ms. Seitz stated that the LVPC has also provided 
direct education, technical assistance and public communication support through provided 
trainings for Lehigh Valley local governments, through presentations, webinars and panels like 
at Greater Lehigh Valley Chamber of Commerce events, through the Pennsylvania Chapter of 
5

[page 6]
    
   
the American Planning Association, and to the Northeast PA Legislative Delegation. The LVPC 
has also used LVPC Committee and Commission meetings, radio programming, public-facing 
discussions and municipal coordination meetings to share experiences and best practices. Ms. 
Seitz concluded by stating the value the LVPC is looking to provide through all this work is that 
municipalities do not have to navigate this issue alone and that the LVPC is serving as a 
regional resource, and work is actively happening to prevent any municipality from having a 
development situation that would be detrimental to the community.  
 
Chair Morgan asked for any comments or questions. Commissioners noted the communities in 
the region that did not have data center regulations and asked if there was anything the LVPC 
could do to assist them. Ms. Bradley and Ms. Seitz stated that the LVPC is actively lending 
support and reaching out to communities to assist with this work. Commissioners also 
discussed the number of data center proposals that are currently in and what can be expected 
over the next 5 years as well as if the LVPC knows the types of data centers being proposed, 
the data centers in the region that already exist and the known impact of the data centers in the 
region. Commissioners and LVPC staff discussed the different types of data centers that have 
been proposed in the region and the known uses of some existing data centers in the region 
and Pennsylvania as a whole. Some Commissioners voiced concerns over the environmental 
and community impacts that data centers could cause, especially in terms of switching internal 
use of data centers and if we should be evaluating and regulating different uses within data 
centers based on their impact. For example, cloud storage data center vs. a data center used 
for artificial intelligence and if some data center uses have higher or lower impact. 
Commissioners further discussed the importance of knowing the impacts on all the different 
types of data centers and how impacts should be tracked as well as the importance that 
communities have strong regulations generally for data centers as they noted it is easier to 
manage the impact rather than the specific use of the data center.  
 
ACTION ITEM: Land Use of Regional Significance (LURS) - Project Atlas Data Center 
Revised Plans   
Ms. Seitz presented the staff review for the proposed development of a 410-acre site by 
constructing three data center buildings, each 492,000 square feet for a total of approximately 
1.5 million square feet at 2493 North Cedar Crest Boulevard. Ms. Seitz noted the LVPC had 
previously reviewed an earlier version of this project in January and that the revised plan has 
been reduced in scale, and the applicant has addressed several prior LVPC comments. Ms. 
Seitz also briefed the Commission that as a part of this review, the LVPC convened a 
coordination meeting with the Township and external review agencies to discuss project 
impacts. Ms. Seitz then briefed the Commission on how this review item was discussed earlier 
this week at the Comprehensive Planning Committee meeting, alongside members of the public 
that attended, and the Committee added two comments to the review letter. The first comment 
encourages the Township to explore standards and best practices for vibration, including 
monitoring and enforcement, to minimize disturbance to nearby residents. The second comment 
strongly recommends coordinating project traffic with adjacent school traffic, including bussing 
and student drop-off and pickup, given the site’s proximity to Parkland High School. Ms. Seitz 
then went through the rest of the review comments, noting the comments in the letter that 
address regional preference for preserving agricultural land and the comments addressing 
quality of life impacts such as noise, vibration, lighting, heat, air quality and visual impacts. Ms. 
Seitz noted that following the LVPC’s comments in the previous review, the applicant has 
provided a noise study, increased setbacks, proposed noise walls, and added landscaping, but 
6

[page 7]
    
   
the LVPC recommends the Township determine whether those measures are adequate and 
ensure commitments are documented through the review process. Other comments addressed 
in the staff review letter included environmental, utility and emergency service considerations 
including karst geology, stormwater, wastewater, energy demand, cooling systems, backup 
generators, fuel storage and emergency access as well as transportation comments that 
address traffic, the new roadway proposed, school-area coordination, congested corridors, 
bridge conditions, sidewalks, Electric Vehicle charging, trail connections and construction traffic.  
 
Vice Chair Moritz-Chapelliquen made the motion to accept the staff comments and 
Commissioner Beavers seconded the motion.  
 
Chair Morgan asked for any comments or questions. Commissioners Daniels, Opthof-Cordaro, 
Glickman and Ghai discussed and asked questions about the project and its impacts, noting 
specific discussions regarding the project’s Environmental Impact Assessment, the karst 
geology on the site, noise and vibrations as well as air quality and sensitive receptors. Ms. 
Bradley and Ms. Seitz, along with Mr. Dave Manhardt, South Whitehall Township’s Director of 
Planning, responded to the questions and comments Commissioners and members of the 
public raised regarding this project.  
 
Chair Morgan called for affirmative votes to accept the staff comments. Commissioner 
Fehlinger-Ricker and Commissioner Herman abstained. The motion passed.

FutureLV: The Regional Plan Update 
Ms. Ruggiero and Ms. Myerov presented a FutureLV plan update which included a brief 
overview of Strategy Lab sessions and the overall timeline of the FutureLV process and an 
update of where we are in the process and upcoming next steps. Ms. Ruggiero presented an 
overview of the Strategy lab sessions, noting a total of 165 people participated in the series of 9 
strategy labs which focused on topics such as economy, borough & rural development and land 
use, cities & suburbs development and land use, transportation, arts and culture, environment, 
community infrastructure, care and crisis and trends and future forces. Ms. Ruggiero also noted 
that staff has been compiling the feedback received within the strategy labs into a report, and 
there will be a qualitative dialogue summary on the agenda for Full Commission next month. 
The Summary of the Strategy labs will help provide insight on needs and priorities for the 
regional plan. Ms. Myerov discussed what has been completed as part of the regional plan 
update thus far, which includes completing key plans such as the Congestion Management 
Plan, the Lehigh Valley Housing Supply and Attainability Strategy and the Lehigh Valley Trail 
Connection Strategy. Ms. Myerov then discussed the next steps of the FutureLV update process 
which includes continued refinement of the LVTS Transportation Project Selection Process, 
building on feedback received from PennDOT during last fall’s Transportation Improvements 
Program project selection discussions, Open Call for Infrastructure Projects, Drafting Policy, 
Subregional Meetings, Complete Draft of the Plan by year’s end, coordination, modeling and 
intergovernmental review in early 2027 and plan adoption deadline of June 30, 2027.

Several situations have arisen that require the LVPC  Environment, Comprehensive Planning, 
Transportation and Full Commission meeting packets to be handled differently.  First, the volume 
and complexity of work the LVPC is managing has increased substantially as growth and 
development have increased.  Meeting materials, as a result, have also increased.  The file size 
of each meeting packet is regularly exceeding the limits for most email systems resulting in the 
need to use other file sharing systems.  File sharing systems, such as Dropbox, have inherent IT 
vulnerabilities, including increased risk of computer viruses and malware.  Because of the 
increased IT vulnerabilities, many of the LVPC Commissioners are prohibited from opening 
meeting materials by their IT team.      
To address this issue and ensure that all Commissioners have access to meeting materials , the 
LVPC team needs to change how we share packets with you for the Committees listed above and 
the Full Commission.  As of August 1st, you will receive a meeting reminder email that contains a 
web link to www.lvpc.org. Follow the web link or go directly to www.lvpc.org to access the meeting 
packet.  Meeting packets will not be sent via email attachment going forward. 
We appreciate your cooperation and understanding as we navigate a changing region and our 
response together.  If you have any issues, please reach out to either Beth Ritter-Guth at britter-
guth@lvpc.org or Jacob Weinberg at jweinberg@lvpc.org.

Talking Business with Becky Bradley: Route 22 was 
built for another era, but is essential for ours and the 
next 
By Becky Bradley 
For The Morning Call 
July 3, 2026 at 8:00 AM 
 
Spoiler alert: Love it or hate it, this article is in defense of Route 22. 
If I didn’t lose you after the first sentence, let’s ride. 
Route 22 has a unique ability to incite emotion in Lehigh Valley drivers. For some, it’s 
the terror of merging into speeding traffic with exactly 12 feet of runway. Others are 
moved to avoid the region’s busiest roadway by taking back roads, only to find everyone 
else had the exact same idea. And some find it the perfect excuse for why they’re late 
for work — for the fourth time this week. 
24

[page 25]
Well, I’m here to defend the old gal, now more than 70 years old. She’s the Lehigh 
Valley’s Main Street, carrying people to jobs, schools, shopping and recreation. She just 
needs a little TLC — OK, a lot of TLC. But that’s why we’re in the midst of developing 
the Route 22: Mobility, Safety and Congestion Management Plan, or as I like to call it 
the “What to do with 22” plan. It’s going to provide a clear vision of what we need Route 
22 to be, and what it will take to get there. We expect a draft by the end of the year, but 
as we collect data for the analysis, an interesting picture is emerging. 
This is a very complex road that will require a ton of specific, surgical and layered 
improvements and the corresponding funding to make critical investments. We are six 
months into the planning process and early data suggests that this is a roadway in 
relatively good condition, but with a design from a bygone era, even as it strains to 
handle the pressures of one of the fastest-growing regions in Pennsylvania. 
Here’s some of what we’ve learned, so far: 
• At its busiest interchange at Airport Road, Route 22 handles more than 107,000 
vehicles a day, of which roughly 10,500 are tractor-trailers. No other road in the region 
is close, although Route 33 is gaining. Subtext — it’s mostly single-occupancy 
passenger vehicles using 22 and only 9.8% of total traffic is tractor-trailers. 
• Route 22 really is our Main Street. Just 7% of the vehicles that travel the highway drive 
the entire 23-mile stretch, without getting off. That means 93% of drivers get on or off — 
or both — within the Valley. It’s original name, “Lehigh Valley Thruway,” no longer 
applies. This is no bypass. 
• Over the last five years, 2,170 crashes have happened — 2,050 were on the roadway 
and 117 on ramps. Most were rear-end collisions where drivers ran into the back of the 
vehicle in front of them because of tailgating, speeding or not allowing enough space to 
stop. Fortunately, rear-end crashes are the least likely to cause fatalities. More on bad 
driver behavior in a minute. 
• In a related matter, 24 acceleration lanes and 13 deceleration lanes are not long 
enough, based on today’s standards. This means drivers on acceleration ramps don’t 
have enough runup to easily merge into moving traffic, and those exiting have to begin 
slowing on the roadway, as they enter an exit ramp with a tight turning radius. These 
lanes and ramps are partially to blame for many of the accidents and congestion 
coupled with excessive speeds. 
• Congestion is about more than too many cars. Causes include: 
— 25% from crashes or stopped vehicles 
— 16% from peak hour travel demand 
25

[page 26]
— 4% from weather 
— 38% from a combination of factors 
— 15% from unknown causes. 
— 1% are in a work zone 
— Less than 1% holiday-related travel 
• Ninety-two percent of Route 22 has a road surface that is rated as being in good or 
excellent condition. Drivers, if you think about it, we really don’t encounter potholes or 
ruts. 
• Route 22 has 33 bridges that pass over or under other roadways. Twenty-nine are 
rated in fair condition and the other four are in good condition, primarily because they 
have outdated design. 
Clearly, 22 has issues. But we rely on it more than any of the other 8,234 unique roads 
in the Lehigh Valley, so we need to take care of it. It is the lifeblood of our transportation 
network, economy and community. With the right plan, motivation and money, we can 
greatly improve its safety and congestion. We know it’s going to be expensive, but we 
must get this right. 
And for those of you Route 22 haters, try to imagine where we’d be without her. Let me 
lay one more piece of data on you. Before we had it, you had to pass through 31 traffic 
signals to get across the region. You know how many Route 22 has now? Zero. 
So yes, Route 22 can be frustrating, if we let it be. It can test our patience, our brakes 
and occasionally our vocabulary, if we let it. But it also makes modern life in the Lehigh 
Valley possible. More than 70 years after it opened, Route 22 remains the backbone of 
a region that has grown far beyond what its designers could have imagined. So, we 
need to refine and invest, not whine, complain and generally send negative vibes 
toward a critical asset. 
The challenge before us isn’t how to fix Route 22. It’s whether we’re willing to invest in 
making it safer, smarter and better suited for the next 70 years. That’s what this plan is 
all about and in a few months I’ll be back to share the solutions package, funding need 
and strategy to make it happen. 
Spoiler alert two: You are part of the plan, so expect an ask. 
This is a contributed opinion column. Becky Bradley is executive director of the Lehigh 
Valley Planning Commission. She can be reached at planning@lvpc.org.
