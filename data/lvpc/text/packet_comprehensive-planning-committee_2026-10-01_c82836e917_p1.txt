The proposed amendment establishes regulations for Data Centers and Data Center Accessory 
Uses and permits data centers by conditional use within the I-2 Industrial District. The 
amendment includes standards addressing setbacks, landscaping and screening, noise and 
vibration, water supply, geotechnical conditions, environmental impacts, electric demand, 
emergency management, building design, parking, cooling systems and electronic waste. 
Many of the proposed standards are measurable, directly related to potential land use impacts 
and advance the planning direction of FutureLV: The Regional Plan by proactively addressing 
the land use and infrastructure impacts of an emerging industrial use. Other provisions may 
overlap responsibilities assigned to state, federal or utility regulators or extend beyond the 
regulatory reach of municipalities in Pennsylvania.  
The following provisions generally address legitimate planning concerns, however the LVPC 
recommends that the Borough and Borough Solicitor further evaluate them before enactment to 
ensure that the standards remain within municipal land use authority, are sufficiently objective 
and do not unnecessarily overlap other regulatory systems: 
Electric Utility Capacity, Interconnection and Rates 
The LVPC supports requiring applicants to disclose anticipated electrical demand and identify 
4

[page 5]
the electric infrastructure necessary to serve a proposed data center. This information supports 
infrastructure planning and advancing sustainability (of Policies 1.1, 2.5 and 3.4).  
However, proposed §1015(I) requires submission of an Energy Management Plan subject to 
approval by the Borough. The LVPC recommends that the Borough evaluate this requirement 
with the Borough Solicitor because regulation of utility service, electric capacity, interconnection 
and rates principally involves the serving utility, Pennsylvania Public Utility Commission and 
other state and federal regulatory systems. 
A more legally defensible approach may be to require the submission of documentation 
projected maximum electrical demand, identification of proposed on-site substations and other 
infrastructure, documentation demonstrating coordination with the serving utility, and available 
information concerning whether the required service and infrastructure can be provided. 
The LVPC also encourages the Borough to verify whether a completed interconnection 
agreement can reasonably be obtained at the conditional use stage. If utility processes occur 
later in project development, the ordinance could instead require reasonable evidence of utility 
coordination at conditional use and final documentation before the appropriate subsequent 
approval or operation. 
The Borough may wish to retain these items as disclosure requirements and encouragements 
while focusing approval on measurable site and land use impacts. 
Air Emissions and EPA Requirements 
The amendment requires energy generation equipment to minimize air pollutant emissions, 
meet or exceed EPA Tier 4 standards and undergo annual testing and reporting. While the 
LVPC recognizes the Borough's objective of protecting air quality and public health, air 
emissions are substantially regulated through federal and Commonwealth environmental 
programs.  
The LVPC recommends that the Borough Solicitor evaluate whether the zoning ordinance 
should independently establish and enforce EPA emissions standards. An alternative approach 
can be to require applicants to identify proposed generators and fuel sources, disclose 
anticipated testing schedules and operating characteristics, and comply with Borough standards 
addressing the local land use impacts of the equipment, including noise, vibration, location, fuel 
storage and screening. This approach would retain the Borough's ability to evaluate local 
impacts while reducing potential overlap with state and federal environmental regulation. 
Prescriptive Cooling System Requirements 
The LVPC supports the Borough's objective of minimizing water consumption. However, 
§1015(M)(b) specifically requires all water-cooled cooling systems to operate as closed loop 
recirculating systems and prohibits open loop, once-through and single-pass cooling. Because 
cooling technology continues to evolve, the LVPC encourages the Borough to evaluate whether 
the ordinance should regulate the desired performance outcome rather than require one 
particular cooling technology. 
5

[page 6]
Post-Construction Sound Verification and Financial Security 
The LVPC supports requiring an as-built sound study because actual operating conditions may 
differ from modeled conditions. Continued compliance with the Borough's established noise 
limits is appropriately enforceable through zoning. However, proposed §1015(E)(a)(3) requires 
the as-built study six months after issuance of the certificate of occupancy and prior to release 
of final escrow for the applicable land development phase. The LVPC recommends that the 
Borough Solicitor evaluate whether long term operational verification can be tied to subdivision 
and land development financial security. Financial security under the MPC generally serves to 
guarantee completion of improvements required through land development approval, while 
continued operational compliance is typically addressed through zoning enforcement. 
Water Resources  
The ordinance references specific Delaware River Basin Commission withdrawal thresholds. 
The LVPC encourages the Borough to consider requiring documentation of all applicable DRBC 
approvals, permits or determinations rather than relying solely on thresholds written into the 
zoning ordinance. This would reduce the potential for the Borough ordinance to become 
inconsistent with future changes to outside regulatory programs. 
Enhanced Geotechnical Investigation 
The proposed ordinance addresses legitimate concerns including carbonate geology, karst 
conditions, mine workings, settlement, groundwater and impacts from excavation and vibration.  
However, §1015(G) requires the applicant to submit an enhanced geotechnical investigation for 
the entire development site before conditional use approval and prescribes detailed 
investigation and construction-related requirements. The LVPC recommends that the Borough 
evaluate whether the enhanced level of investigation should be triggered by objective evidence 
of potentially hazardous conditions, such as mapped carbonate geology or karst, known mines, 
unstable slopes, documented subsurface voids, or other identified geological hazards. This 
approach would maintain strong protection where geotechnical risks are present while more 
clearly tying the requirement to site specific land use conditions. 
The LVPC encourages the Borough Solicitor and Engineer to evaluate the proposed ordinance 
for land suitability information needed for conditional use review, and detailed structural design 
requirements governed through applicable construction codes. 
Environmental Impact Assessment and Approval Standards 
The LVPC supports requiring applicants to evaluate potential impacts involving noise, lighting, 
stormwater, natural resources and other environmental conditions. However, the required 
Environmental Impact Assessment also includes broad concepts such as probable future 
effects, regional effects, alternatives analysis and consistency with municipal and county 
comprehensive plans. 
The LVPC recommends that the Borough evaluate whether each element of the Environmental 
Impact Assessment clearly informs compliance with an objective ordinance standard or 
identifies mitigation that the Borough is legally authorized to require. In particular, 
comprehensive plan consistency is valuable planning information, but the Borough should 
6

[page 7]
evaluate whether inconsistency with a comprehensive plan can independently determine 
approval of a use that the zoning ordinance expressly permits by conditional use. 
The LVPC encourages the Borough to use the Environmental Impact Assessment primarily as a 
tool to identify impacts and appropriate mitigation under express ordinance standards. 
Subjective Standards 
Several provisions use standards such as an “unacceptable risk” to public health, safety, 
welfare or infrastructure, or require emergency procedures to be “sufficiently protective” of 
public health and safety. These concepts reflect legitimate municipal concerns, but the LVPC 
recommends that the Borough evaluate whether they can be connected more directly to 
measurable ordinance requirements, applicable professional standards or findings by qualified 
professionals. Clear and objective criteria make the conditional use process easier to 
administer, provide applicants and residents with more predictable expectations and strengthen 
the Borough's ability to defend and enforce its decisions. 
Technical Study Preparation 
The ordinance appropriately requires technical studies to be prepared by professionals with 
qualifications relevant to the work. However, the ordinance states that a professional is not 
independent if “employed by” the applicant, developer or landowner. Because applicants 
ordinarily retain and compensate the engineers, acoustical professionals and other consultants 
preparing required studies, the LVPC recommends clarifying this provision. Submission of 
technical studies that the applicant is required to prepare does not mean that the Borough must 
accept the findings without scrutiny. 
Permit Revocation for Noise or Vibration Violations 
The ordinance authorizes the Borough to revoke previously issued zoning permits where noise 
or vibration violations are not remediated (§1015(E)(c)). The LVPC recommends that the 
Borough Solicitor evaluate the proposed permit revocation mechanism against corrective action 
and other remedies available under the MPC, Pennsylvania court case precedent, and the 
Borough’s ordinance. 
FutureLV Alignment 
Several provisions of the proposed amendment support best planning practices and advance 
policies of FutureLV: The Regional Plan by: 
• Permitting data centers within an industrial district and evaluating infrastructure needs 
supports guiding the location and intensity of development and matching development 
with sustainable infrastructure capacity (of Policy 1.1). 
• Electrical demand disclosure and infrastructure coordination support preparing electric 
utility infrastructure for future technologies and jobs (of Policy 2.5). 
• Water supply analysis, stormwater considerations and environmental review support 
conservation of natural resources and minimization of development impacts (of Policies 
3.1 and 3.2).

• Measurable noise and vibration standards, landscaping and equipment screening help 
protect surrounding properties and community quality of life (of Policies 3.2 and 5.4). 
• Emergency response planning and coordination support safe and resilient communities 
(of Policy 5.1). 
• Building articulation, landscaping and objective site design requirements promote 
context-sensitive design (of Policy 5.4). 
• Clear, measurable and legally supportable approval standards support an efficient and 
predictable development process (of Policy 1.4). 
The LVPC has copied representatives of the River Central Multi-Municipal Comprehensive Plan 
to ‘coordinate land use decisions across municipal boundaries’ (of Policy 1.4).

The amendment replaces Ordinance No. 2026-2 and establishes Article 1400 for Digital 
Infrastructure Facilities. It distinguishes residential and other accessory computing systems from 
principal Edge Computing Facilities and Hyper Digital Infrastructure Facilities, permits principal 
facilities by conditional use in the Regional Use Overlay District 2 (RUO-2), and addresses 
infrastructure, water, noise, screening, emergency response, thermal impacts and ongoing 
reporting. 
The intent of the ordinance amendment advances FutureLV: The Regional Plan by addressing 
the impacts of an emerging land use on the environment, infrastructure, and community. 
 Because several provisions implicate authority assigned by state law to other agencies, or 
extend beyond customary zoning performance standards, the LVPC offers the following 
recommendations to further strengthen the amendment and ensure that its protections remain 
legally defensible under Pennsylvania law: 
 
9

[page 10]
  
   
 
Noise Limits 
The proposed ‘Sound Level Limits by Receiving Land Use/District’ table (§917.05) allows an 80 
dBA Maximum Sound Level during the day and 65 dBA at night, at lot lines of a lot in a 
residential district. While 65 dBA is similar to the noise level of household appliances 
(refrigerator, dishwasher, etc.), 80 dBA is similar to the level of a busy highway or gas 
lawnmower and at a continuous level would have a substantial impact on neighboring 
properties. To ‘minimize impacts of development’ (of Policy 3.2), the LVPC recommends that 
the Township evaluate lower limits appropriate to continuous operations and existing residential 
conditions.  
The proposed ordinance includes a section that allows an exception from the maximum decibel 
levels in cases of power outages when backup generators are being used (§1412(D)), but this 
should be reconciled with §917.03, which states that no emergency generators shall exceed the 
specified sound levels. 
The table also assigns both limits to Sundays and national holidays, creating conflicting 
requirements, and should be clarified. 
Electronic Waste Plan 
The proposed ordinance requires applicants to submit an Electronic Waste Plan (§1418.03), 
which supports responsible electronic and hazardous waste management (of Policy 3.2). 
However, stating that the Electronic Waste Plan is subject to approval by the Township should 
be evaluated with the Township Solicitor to ensure that the approval provision does not overstep 
the Township’s purview. 
Utility Capacity and Approvals 
§1407.01 of the proposed ordinance addresses Electrical Demand Disclosure, Infrastructure 
Capacity, and Impact Standards. Requiring applicants to disclose the anticipated electrical 
demand and supporting infrastructure of the facility supports ‘expanding and reinforcing electric 
utility capacity’ (of Policy 2.5). However, §1407.01.B. states that no facilities shall be approved 
unless the applicant demonstrates that adequate infrastructure capacity exists, and the LVPC 
recommends clarifying that the Township evaluates site impacts and relies on utility findings 
rather than independently determining grid adequacy. Aspects of utility regulation may fall under 
the jurisdiction of the Pennsylvania Public Utility Commission and legally may not be subject to 
local zoning approval. 
Water Study and DRBC Requirements 
The LVPC supports evaluating water demand, supply availability and potential impacts on water 
resources. However, the proposed ordinance (§1408.02(A)) requires the same Water 
Resources Impact Statement regardless of whether the facility uses public water or a private 
well. Certain requirements such as the hydrogeological study and evaluation of nearby wells, 
surface waters and groundwater may not be relevant to every proposal. The LVPC recommends 
that the Township and Township Solicitor distinguish information required for all proposals from 
additional studies triggered by the proposed water source, withdrawal location, demand and 
potential impacts. For public water proposals, documentation from the provider confirming 
adequate supply and delivery capacity may address some requirements and support matching 
10

[page 11]
  
   
 
development intensity with sustainable infrastructure capacity (of Policy 1.1). Additional 
hydrogeological investigations should be tied to identified risks and relevant withdrawal 
locations, which may differ from the facility site. This approach would maintain resource 
protections while making submission requirements more proportional and legally supportable. 
Section 1408.02(D) includes references water withdrawal gallons-per-day thresholds that would 
require proof of review and approval from the Delaware River Basin Commission (DRBC). 
Rather than directly stating thresholds which may or may not align with DRBC (current policies 
and if policies change), requiring DRBC documentation would be clearer and more enforceable. 
This would support protecting water resources while encouraging an efficient development 
process responsive to regional needs (of Policies 3.2 and 1.4). 
The LVPC also recommends that the Township Solicitor evaluate whether the Township can 
require outside-agency approval in its review process (§1408.02(D)). Recent Pennsylvania 
Commonwealth Court cases have indicated that municipalities are limited in their ability to allow 
outside agency processes to impact local processes, such as final plan signatures or building 
permit processing (PC Land LLC v. Board of Commissioners of Bethlehem Township, 320 A.3d 
804 (PA Commonwealth 2024)). Requiring identification of applicable DRBC requirements and 
documentation of application status could preserve agency permitting obligations without 
making their completion a blanket prerequisite to Township approval. Clear approval 
sequencing and coordination between municipal and agency reviews would further support an 
efficient development process (of Policy 1.4). 
Air Emissions and Generator Technology 
The proposed ordinance addresses air quality standards related to backup power equipment, 
and requires annual testing and reports provided to the Township to ensure that emissions do 
not exceed allowable limits (§1411). Air emissions are regulated by the Pennsylvania 
Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 
(EPA). The LVPC recommends that the Township Solicitor evaluate the legal basis and 
enforcement procedures around these provisions as a best practice when regulating land uses 
(or Policy 1.4). 
Construction Codes 
The proposed ordinance includes code-compliance references for specific equipment, 
ventilation, monitoring and fire-protection requirements (§1405L, N-O, 1408.08 and 1417). The 
LVPC recommends distinguishing zoning standards for location, access and compatibility from 
construction and installation standards administered through the Pennsylvania Uniform 
Construction Code (UCC). The Township should evaluate any requirements exceeding the UCC 
through the procedures applicable under the Pennsylvania Construction Code Act Section 503, 
governing how municipalities can change, modify, or exceed the state’s UCC as a best practice 
when regulating land uses (or Policy 1.4). 
Thermal Impact Mitigation 
The LVPC supports requiring applicants to document anticipated heat generation, cooling 
equipment, exhaust locations, operating conditions and potential impacts on neighboring 
properties to minimize environmental impacts of development (of Policy 3.2). However, the 
11

[page 12]
  
   
 
proposed ordinance includes cooling technology restrictions and authorizes the Township to 
require alternative cooling systems, construction materials and other design measures 
(§1405(G), 1415 and 1422(5)–(6)). The LVPC recommends that the Township Solicitor and 
code officials evaluate provisions that extend beyond impact assessment and mitigation 
planning into equipment specifications or construction methods, which may overlap with the 
Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code. Prescriptive requirements may also limit the use of 
emerging technologies that achieve greater water and energy efficiency or reduce 
environmental impacts. The Township is encouraged to focus on measurable site impacts and 
allow applicants to demonstrate effective mitigation through professionally supported 
approaches as a best practice when regulating land uses (or Policy 1.4). 
Stormwater System Design Standard 1408.07 
The LVPC recommends that the proposed section on Stormwater Standards (§1408.07) include 
cross reference to the Township’s Stormwater Management Ordinance to ensure that 
stormwater control measures comply with the stormwater standards and criteria (of Policy 3.2).  
Post-Construction Verification and Financial Security 
The proposed ordinance ties post-construction sound studies to final land development escrow 
release (§1412(A)(3)). The LVPC recommends evaluating this linkage against Sections 509 and 
510 of the MPC, which governs security for required improvements and its release. Completed 
sound barriers and other required improvements can be verified through the applicable 
improvement process, but ongoing operational noise compliance should be addressed through 
enforceable zoning standards. 
Reporting and Mandatory Public Dashboard 
The proposed ordinance requires annual reports and a publicly accessible dashboard with 
monthly utility data, energy-source information and three years of records (§1423(1) and (3)). 
The LVPC supports information needed to verify water, noise and operating limits, but 
recommends evaluating the authority and proportionality of mandatory public reporting. 
Automatic Shutdown and Corrective Action 
The proposed ordinance requires discontinued operations when reports exceed original water 
estimates or noise limits (§1423(2)). The automatic shutdown requirement and cease-and-desist 
authority in §1412(A)(3) should be evaluated against the MPC’s requirements for enforcement 
notice, appeal and judicial remedy procedures (MPC Sections 616.1, 617 and 617.2) as a best 
practice when regulating land uses (or Policy 1.4).

infrastructure capacity’ (of Policy 1.1). Full build-out and phasing disclosures and identifying 
separate developments that are integrated supports evaluation of cumulative site impacts.

The application proposes amending the City’s Zoning Ordinance and Subdivision and Land 
Development Ordinance to clean up sections of the ordinances, improve administrative clarity, 
add new uses, codify elements of existing patterns in the City, and cover regulatory gaps 
identified in the ordinances by staff and planning commissioners. The LVPC strongly supports 
the City’s efforts to update its ordinances through “facilitated discussion among residents, 
stakeholders, appointed officials and elected representatives” (of Policy 1.4). The substantial 
update represents the achievement of many goals outlined in FutureLV: The Regional Plan by 
“integrating efficiency measures and emerging technologies” and “supporting evolution and 
adaptability of government” (of Policy 1.1). 
 
Clarifying Amendments 
The proposed ordinance amendment adds or removes several sections that could create 
conflicts within the Zoning Ordinance, or whose removal would add clarity. These efforts support 
“an efficient development process responsive to regional needs” (of Policy 1.4) by making it 
easier for residents, developers, and officials to use the Zoning Ordinance. Some improvements 
include making sure that all parking requirements are in the off-street parking requirements 
section of the ordinance rather than in their specific districts, centralizing specific shopping 
location provisions in the supplemental regulations, and clarifying the rules for measuring 
setbacks, density, lot coverage, off-street parking, and the distance for the separation of uses. 
14

[page 15]
 
 
 
Codification of Existing Patterns 
The proposed ordinance also codifies several existing patterns in the City. Changes include 
separating principal uses on one lot, changing fence height limits, adjusting driveway widths, 
adjusting setback standards to consider alignment with existing buildings, lowering building 
heights to be compatible with existing buildings, and clarifying regulations on the frequency of 
convenience stores next to existing retail. These changes all help “promote development that 
complements the unique history, environment, culture and needs of the valley” by “promoting 
context specific design solutions” (of Policy 5.4). 
 
Map Changes 
As a part of the codification changes two different areas of the zoning map: 
 
The first area was rezoned from the River Corridor (RC) district, which serves as the City’s 
environmentally sensitive zoning area due to its proximity to the river, to the West Ward (WW) 
district. It lies between North 7th Street, Bushkill Street, and Veile Street. Mapping does indicate 
the presence of steep slopes and a riparian buffer area along the Bushkill Creek, but the 
15

[page 16]
 
 
existing form of the neighborhood aligns better with the West Ward Zoning District. The LVPC 
supports the City exploring, in future updates, the inclusion of a riparian buffer overlay, which 
would allow the City to protect its waterways, “protecting high-priority natural lands and 
resources” (of Policy 3.1) while “promoting an efficient development process responsive to 
regional needs” (of Policy 1.4). 
 
The second area, between Centre Street, West Cooper Street, Pastor Fred Davis Street, and 
West Canal Street, changes from the South Side (SS) district to the Adaptive Reuse (AR) 
district. The current district splits the Adaptive Reuse District, and connecting the two creates a 
corridor where existing uses and the promoted multi-family and institutional uses would fit well 
with neighborhood character, which would “guide location and intensity of development” and 
“encourage reuse and redevelopment in urban areas” (of Policy 1.1). 
 
New Uses 
The proposed ordinance also adds seventeen new uses. Several of these are split out from 
uses already codified and have greater impacts. The ordinance separates a regular bar and pub 
from a night club, a crematorium from a burial place/cemetery, truck terminals from warehouses, 
entertainment and gaming establishments from gambling establishments, and convenience 
stores and grocery stores from normal retail uses. These uses are often more impact-intensive 
and are given supplemental regulations that help mitigate these impacts. The changes help 
“minimize impacts of Land Uses of Regional Significance” (of Policy 1.4). Many of the proposed 
regulations help “minimize the environmental impact of development” and place these uses in 
zones that fit their character, which helps “promote development that complements the unique 
history, environment, culture and needs of the valley” (of Policy 5.4). Several of these changes 
advance the policies and goals of FutureLV: The Regional Plan. 
 
Electric Vehicle Charging 
The ordinance allows electric vehicle charging both as a principal use and as an accessory use, 
and makes the accessory use widely available throughout many of the districts in the City where 
appropriate. These provisions help “advance deployment of advanced fueling infrastructure” and 
“incorporate environmentally sensitive transportation technologies” (of Policies 2.5 and 3.4). 
 
Solar 
The ordinance also seeks to change how solar energy systems are regulated in the City. It 
removes sections that are covered by other legislation and are not powers given to zoning 
under the MPC, as well as sections that make installing solar more restrictive and factors that 
should be determined by the installer. Easing these regulations helps make solar more 
accessible to residents and helps “reduce climate change impacts through mitigation and 
adaptation” (of Policy 3.4). The ordinance also includes a provision reducing the fine for

$500, with each day constituting an additional violation. 
 
Warehouse and Truck Terminal Standards 
The ordinance proposes new regulations for warehouse/logistics uses that help “strengthen 
freight mobility to minimize quality of life impacts to residents” (of Policy 2.4). The LVPC 
supports the inclusion of snow removal equipment for trucks; electric charging infrastructure, 
which supports Policy 2.5; bicycle racks, which support Policies 2.2 and 5.2; native landscaping 
and natural resource preservation, which support Policy 3.4; and truck driver amenities. The 
truck terminal use must also meet all of those requirements, as well as additional parking 
amenities and signage requirements that strengthen Policy 2.4 for freight-exclusive uses.

Data Centers 
The ordinance also adds regulations for data centers in the City. The proposed amendment 
includes many strong data center provisions and addresses concerns related to impacts, 
including thermal impact mitigation plans, environmental impact assessments to measure water, 
sewer, and electrical impacts of the development, electronic waste decommissioning plans, 
emergency response plans, buffering, and setbacks. Because data centers operate 24/7 and 
noise levels are unlikely to change throughout the day, the LVPC recommends that the City 
consider setting one allowable standard each for dBA and dBC. 
 
Subdivision and Land Development Ordinance 
The ordinance proposes several changes to the Subdivision and Land Development Ordinance. 
It adds requirements for transportation impact studies and transportation impact assessments, 
which help “provide a safe, well-maintained transportation network” (of Policy 2.2) and “promote 
use of cost-benefit analysis in planning, development and infrastructure decisions” (of Policy 
4.6). The ordinance also adds a general impact assessment split into two sections: a community 
impact assessment, based on the impact of the use on surrounding uses and its compatibility 
with the built environment, and an environmental impact assessment, which details how the 
proposed use would affect the environment where it is built. These assessments help “minimize 
impacts of Land Uses of Regional Significance” (of Policy 1.4) and “minimize the environmental 
impact of development” (of Policy 3.2).
